## How to use this bank

Do not treat an example as a ready-made essay point. Ask:

1. **What does this evidence show?**
2. **What does it not prove?**
3. **What could it be compared with?**
4. **Does it show change, continuity, cause, consequence or significance?**
5. **For a source, what makes it valuable for this exact enquiry?**

For Unit 3, evidence should normally be spread across the chronology so that a judgement covers the whole period rather than one famous decade.

For Unit 4, primary sources should be evaluated through **content + context + provenance + the exact enquiry**. A source is not automatically weak because it is partisan, and a government source is not automatically reliable.

For Unit 5, this bank supports research method only. Kellyn must select, analyse and write her assessed enquiry herself.

# Part A - Unit 3 Option 8: The American Century, c.1890-1990

WJEC defines two main themes across the whole period: **The struggle for Civil Rights** and **The Making of a Superpower**. Breadth questions require sustained comparison across time.

Source: [WJEC GCE History specification](https://www.wjec.co.uk/media/uwlondvv/wjec-gce-history-spec-from-2015-e.pdf)

## 1. US government, federalism and the machinery of change

### H1 - Plessy v Ferguson, 1896

**Best fit:** Supreme Court, federalism, continuity of segregation

The Supreme Court upheld racial segregation under the "separate but equal" doctrine. The decision helped give constitutional protection to segregation for decades.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the Supreme Court could preserve racial inequality as well as dismantle it;
- federal institutions were not consistently progressive;
- a century-long judgement needs to compare the Court before and after Brown.

**Source:** [US National Archives - Brown v Board lesson, including Plessy context](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/brown-v-board)

### H2 - Brown v Board of Education, 1954

**Best fit:** Supreme Court, federal power, legal turning point

The Supreme Court ruled state-sponsored segregation in public schools unconstitutional.

**Short primary quotation:**

> "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal"

**What this demonstrates:**

- constitutional interpretation could overturn a long-standing legal settlement;
- Brown was a major legal turning point but did not by itself integrate every school;
- legal change and implementation must be separated.

**Source:** [US National Archives - Brown v Board of Education](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/brown-v-board-of-education)

### H3 - Little Rock, 1957

**Best fit:** President versus state government, enforcement of federal law

When Arkansas authorities resisted school desegregation at Central High School, President Eisenhower federalised the Arkansas National Guard and sent federal troops to enforce the court order.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Supreme Court rulings sometimes required executive enforcement;
- federalism could create direct conflict between state and national authority;
- presidential importance can be measured by implementation, not just speeches.

**Source:** [US National Park Service - Brown and the modern civil-rights movement](https://www.nps.gov/brvb/planyourvisit/the-story-of-brown.htm)

### H4 - Civil Rights Act 1964 and Voting Rights Act 1965

**Best fit:** Congress, Johnson, federal legislation

The Civil Rights Act prohibited discrimination in major areas of public life and employment. The Voting Rights Act attacked discriminatory voting practices including literacy tests and gave the federal government stronger enforcement powers.

**What this demonstrates:**

- major change often depended on interaction between protest, presidency and Congress;
- legislation could reach areas that court rulings alone had not transformed;
- Johnson's role should be compared with grassroots activism and earlier legal challenges.

**Sources:** [National Archives - Civil Rights Act 1964](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/civil-rights-act) | [National Archives - Voting Rights Act 1965](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/voting-rights-act)

# 2. The struggle for Civil Rights, c.1890-1990

### H5 - Jim Crow and the Plessy settlement

**Best fit:** 1890-1945 continuity, legal barriers

By the turn of the twentieth century, segregation and disfranchisement were entrenched across much of the South. Plessy gave the system constitutional protection.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the starting point for the breadth theme was severe legal and political exclusion;
- later progress should be judged against how deeply embedded the earlier system was.

**Source:** [Library of Congress - The Segregation Era](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/civil-rights-act/segregation-era.html)

### H6 - The Great Migration

**Best fit:** migration, change before 1945, political influence

From the 1910s to 1970, millions of African Americans moved from the South to northern and western urban areas. World War I employment opportunities, transport links, Jim Crow and racial violence all contributed.

**What this demonstrates:**

- civil-rights change did not begin in the 1950s;
- migration changed economic opportunities, political voting strength and the geography of African American life;
- it also shows why change could coexist with continued discrimination in northern cities.

**Source:** [Library of Congress - The Great Migration](https://www.loc.gov/classroom-materials/great-migration)

### H7 - NAACP legal campaigning

**Best fit:** organisations, long-term strategy

The NAACP used federal courts to challenge disenfranchisement, housing segregation and later educational segregation. Its legal work built over decades before Brown.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Brown was not an isolated event;
- sustained institutional strategy mattered alongside mass protest;
- different organisations used different routes to change.

**Source:** [Library of Congress - The Segregation Era](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/civil-rights-act/segregation-era.html)

### H8 - Double V Campaign and the Second World War

**Best fit:** war as catalyst, 1941-1945

The Double V Campaign connected victory over fascism abroad with victory over racism at home. The NAACP supported the campaign and wartime mobilisation increased pressure against racial discrimination.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the Second World War could expose contradictions between US war aims and domestic segregation;
- wartime employment, military service and activism accelerated demands for change;
- war was a catalyst, not the only cause of later civil-rights progress.

**Source:** [Library of Congress - NAACP during World War II](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/naacp/world-war-ii-and-the-post-war-years.html)

### H9 - Montgomery Bus Boycott, 1955-1956

**Best fit:** grassroots protest, Rosa Parks, King, sustained mobilisation

Rosa Parks' arrest helped trigger a boycott involving about 17,000 Black citizens. The campaign lasted more than a year and ended with bus segregation being struck down.

**What this demonstrates:**

- grassroots organisation and economic pressure could achieve practical change;
- Rosa Parks was already an activist, so the event should not be reduced to a spontaneous individual act;
- local action could combine with legal challenge.

**Source:** [US National Park Service - Montgomery Bus Boycott](https://home.nps.gov/articles/montgomery-bus-boycott.htm)

### H10 - Brown to the Civil Rights Act

**Best fit:** pace of change, turning points

Brown in 1954 challenged school segregation. The Civil Rights Act in 1964 prohibited discrimination across a much wider range of public and employment settings.

**What this demonstrates:**

- change was cumulative rather than produced by one event;
- a ten-year comparison can show movement from constitutional principle towards broader statutory enforcement.

**Sources:** [National Archives - Brown](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/brown-v-board-of-education) | [National Archives - Civil Rights Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/civil-rights-act)

### H11 - Voting Rights Act, 1965

**Best fit:** political rights, federal enforcement

The Act outlawed discriminatory voting practices and gave federal examiners powers in covered jurisdictions.

**What this demonstrates:**

- legal political rights required enforcement mechanisms;
- Selma and violent resistance helped build national pressure;
- success should be judged by both legal change and participation in practice.

**Source:** [National Archives - Voting Rights Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/voting-rights-act)

### H12 - Civil rights after 1968

**Best fit:** continuity after formal legal victories

The WJEC theme continues to 1990 because formal desegregation and voting rights did not remove every economic, residential, educational or political inequality. Black Power, affirmative action, urban unrest and debates over desegregation all belong in the later period.

**What this demonstrates:**

- 1964-65 can be a major turning point without being an endpoint;
- a strong breadth answer distinguishes legal equality from lived equality.

**Source:** [WJEC History specification](https://www.wjec.co.uk/media/uwlondvv/wjec-gce-history-spec-from-2015-e.pdf)

# 3. The Making of a Superpower, c.1890-1990

### H13 - Spanish-American War, 1898

**Best fit:** imperialism, growing global reach

US victory over Spain led to control or influence in Cuba and acquisition of Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines, while Hawaii was annexed during the conflict.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the USA was already moving beyond continental power before 1900;
- the war is strong evidence against describing the whole pre-1941 era as simple isolationism.

**Source:** [US Office of the Historian - Spanish-American War](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1866-1898/spanish-american-war)

### H14 - Panama Canal, 1903-1914

**Best fit:** strategic and commercial power

The Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty gave the USA control of a canal zone, and the completed canal transformed movement between the Atlantic and Pacific.

**What this demonstrates:**

- US power combined military, commercial and geopolitical interests;
- the canal symbolised growing technological and strategic reach.

**Source:** [US Office of the Historian - Building the Panama Canal](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1899-1913/panama-canal)

### H15 - US entry into the First World War and the Fourteen Points

**Best fit:** neutrality versus engagement, Wilson

The USA entered the war in April 1917. Wilson's Fourteen Points then proposed principles including open diplomacy, self-determination and a League of Nations.

**Short primary quotation:**

> "a just and secure peace"

**What this demonstrates:**

- military entry showed growing capacity, while Wilsonianism showed an ambition to shape the post-war order;
- later US refusal to join the League shows the limits of this internationalist turn.

**Source:** [US Office of the Historian - Wilson's Fourteen Points](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/fourteen-points)

### H16 - Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor

**Best fit:** limits of isolationism, Roosevelt

Before formal entry into the Second World War, Roosevelt expanded material support for Britain and other Allies through measures including Lend-Lease.

**What this demonstrates:**

- legal neutrality did not mean complete political or economic detachment;
- foreign policy changed gradually before December 1941 rather than overnight.

**Source:** [US Office of the Historian - Lend-Lease](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/lend-lease)

### H17 - Truman Doctrine, 1947

**Best fit:** containment, Cold War turning point

Truman asked Congress for aid to Greece and Turkey and presented a wider principle of supporting peoples resisting external or armed pressure.

**Short primary quotation:**

> "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation"

**What this demonstrates:**

- post-war US foreign policy accepted continuing global commitments;
- containment marked a much deeper break from inter-war reluctance to permanent political involvement.

**Source:** [US National Archives - Truman Doctrine](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/truman-doctrine)

### H18 - Marshall Plan, 1948

**Best fit:** economic power, containment, Western Europe

Congress provided $13.3 billion for European recovery over four years.

**What this demonstrates:**

- superpower status rested on economic as well as military power;
- the USA used aid to rebuild markets, support political stability and reinforce the Western bloc.

**Source:** [US National Archives - Marshall Plan](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/marshall-plan)

### H19 - Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962

**Best fit:** Kennedy, nuclear superpower competition

For thirteen days the USA and USSR confronted each other over Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba, bringing the world close to nuclear war.

**What this demonstrates:**

- superpower status created global influence but also extreme risk;
- presidential crisis management, military strength and diplomacy all mattered.

**Source:** [US National Archives - Cuban Missile Crisis](https://www.archives.gov/news/topics/cuban-missile-crisis)

### H20 - Détente and arms control

**Best fit:** 1970s change in method, Nixon/Ford/Carter

Détente reduced some tensions and produced arms-control diplomacy, but competition continued and relations worsened again after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.

**What this demonstrates:**

- détente changed the management of rivalry rather than ending the Cold War;
- continuity and change can operate at the same time.

**Source:** [US Office of the Historian - Détente and Arms Control](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/detente)

### H21 - Reagan, Gorbachev and the end of the Cold War

**Best fit:** 1980s, arms talks, change in US-Soviet relations

Reagan moved from confrontation towards negotiations with Gorbachev. The INF Treaty was signed in 1987 and US-Soviet relations transformed rapidly in the later 1980s.

**Short primary quotation:**

> "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"

**What this demonstrates:**

- presidential leadership mattered, but should be weighed alongside Gorbachev's reforms, Soviet economic weakness and wider changes in Eastern Europe;
- crediting Reagan alone is too simplistic for a high-level judgement.

**Sources:** [Ronald Reagan Presidential Library - 1987 Berlin speech](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/major-speeches-1964-1989/major-presidential-speeches-second-term-1985-1989) | [US Office of the Historian - US-Soviet Relations 1981-1991](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1981-1988/u.s.-soviet-relations)

# 4. Unit 3 Breadth Judgement Bank

### H22 - Testing a turning point: 1941

Compare US policy before Pearl Harbor with Lend-Lease, wartime mobilisation and the permanent post-1945 commitments of the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan.

**What this demonstrates:** A turning point is strongest when the change is large, durable and alters later policy.

### H23 - Testing a turning point: 1954-1965

Compare Brown, Montgomery, Little Rock, the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act.

**What this demonstrates:** A turning point may be a process involving courts, grassroots mobilisation, Congress and presidents rather than one date.

### H24 - Testing continuity

Compare Plessy-era legal exclusion with post-1968 inequalities.

**What this demonstrates:** Major legal progress can coexist with continuity in social and economic outcomes.

### H25 - Testing presidential importance

Compare Wilson, Roosevelt, Truman, Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon and Reagan against other forces such as Congress, courts, activists, war, economic power and Soviet decisions.

**What this demonstrates:** A president can be decisive in one phase without being the single most important factor across a century.

# Part B - Unit 4 Option 3: Protest and Campaigns for Social Reform, c.1832-1848

## 5. Whig reform and state intervention

### H26 - Factory Act 1833

**Best fit:** factory reform, humanitarianism, enforcement

The Act prohibited factory work for children under nine, limited hours for older children and created a small factory inspectorate.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the state was beginning to regulate industrial conditions more directly;
- enforcement matters: only four inspectors initially covered thousands of mills;
- humanitarian concern and administrative control can both form part of the explanation.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - The 1833 Factory Act](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/overview/factoryact/)

### H27 - Poor Law Amendment Act 1834

**Best fit:** utilitarian reform, economy, social control, opposition

The Act reorganised poor relief under greater central control and encouraged workhouse provision designed to deter unnecessary claims.

**What this demonstrates:**

- reform did not always mean a more generous state;
- efficiency, economy and deterrence were central motives;
- the Act itself became a cause of protest.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Poor Law reform](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/overview/poorlaw/)

### H28 - Municipal Corporations Act 1835

**Best fit:** local government reform

The Act replaced many unreformed municipal corporations with elected borough councils and began a more standardised system of local government.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Whig reform was political and administrative as well as social;
- local government capacity was important to later public-health reform.

**Source:** [House of Commons Library - History of local government](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10233/CBP-10233.pdf)

### H29 - Mines and Collieries Act 1842

**Best fit:** social investigation, Shaftesbury, child and female labour

A Royal Commission exposed conditions in mines. The resulting Act prohibited underground work for women and girls and boys under ten.

**What this demonstrates:**

- investigation and public exposure could turn hidden conditions into political pressure;
- reform could be rapid when parliamentary concern and campaigning aligned.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Coal mines](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/overview/coalmines/)

### H30 - Factory Act 1844 and Ten Hours Act 1847

**Best fit:** continuity of reform, limitations of earlier Acts

The 1844 Act introduced stronger safety rules and working-hour limits. The 1847 Act established a ten-hour day for women and young people aged 13-18.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the 1833 Act was an important beginning but not a complete solution;
- reform developed through repeated legislation because loopholes and enforcement problems remained.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Later factory legislation](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/overview/laterfactoryleg/)

### H31 - Chadwick and Public Health Act 1848

**Best fit:** public health, utilitarianism, industrial towns

Edwin Chadwick's 1842 sanitary report linked disease and poor sanitation with the economic costs of poverty. The 1848 Public Health Act created a national framework for local sanitary action.

**What this demonstrates:**

- humanitarian and economic motives could reinforce one another;
- public-health reform expanded the idea that government had responsibilities for urban conditions.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - The 1848 Public Health Act](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/towncountry/towns/tyne-and-wear-case-study/about-the-group/public-administration/the-1848-public-health-act/)

# 6. Chartism

### H32 - The People's Charter, 1838

**Best fit:** aims and political programme

The Charter called for six reforms including universal male suffrage, secret ballots, equal constituencies, annual elections, payment of MPs and removal of the property qualification for MPs.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Chartism had a clear national political programme;
- later reform can be measured against these specific demands.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - The Chartist movement](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/chartists/overview/chartistmovement/)

### H33 - First petition and rejection, 1839

**Best fit:** mass mobilisation, parliamentary resistance

The first national Chartist petition had more than 1.25 million signatures but was rejected by Parliament.

**What this demonstrates:**

- popular mobilisation did not translate directly into parliamentary power;
- rejection helped intensify debates within Chartism about moral and physical force.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - The Chartist movement](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/chartists/overview/chartistmovement/)

### H34 - Newport Rising, 4 November 1839

**Best fit:** Welsh Chartism, physical force, government response

Thousands of Chartists marched into Newport under leaders including John Frost. Troops at the Westgate Hotel opened fire and at least 22 protesters were killed.

**What this demonstrates:**

- South Wales was central to national Chartist history;
- the event shows both the depth of support and the state's capacity to crush armed protest;
- it should not be used to imply that all Chartists favoured violence.

**Sources:** [The National Archives - People's Charter and Newport](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/on-the-record-podcast/on-the-record-the-peoples-charter/) | [National Library of Wales - social unrest and Newport](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/printed-material/the-blue-books-of-1847)

### H35 - The 1842 petition

**Best fit:** scale of support, class legislation

The second petition carried about 3.3 million signatures, around a third of the adult population, but Parliament again rejected the Charter.

**Short primary quotation:**

> "all should be equally represented"

**What this demonstrates:**

- Chartism could mobilise extraordinary national support;
- numerical support alone did not overcome parliamentary opposition.

**Sources:** [UK Parliament - Chartist petition of 1842](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/326/petitions-committee/news/99148/the-chartist-petition-of1842/) | [Historic Hansard - People's Charter petition](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/02/the-peoples-charter-petition)

### H36 - Plug Plot disturbances, 1842

**Best fit:** economic distress, labour protest and Chartism

Workers in industrial districts stopped machinery by removing boiler plugs during strikes linked to wage cuts and economic hardship. Chartist political demands overlapped with industrial protest.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Chartist support rose during periods of economic pressure;
- political and economic grievances could reinforce one another.

**Source:** [The National Archives - What was Chartism?](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/what-was-chartism/)

### H37 - Kennington Common and the third petition, 1848

**Best fit:** decline, government preparedness, limits of mobilisation

The 1848 demonstration attracted far fewer people than organisers hoped. Parliament later disputed the claimed number of petition signatures and the movement lost momentum.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Chartism's organisational strength had limits;
- government preparation and the changing economic/political climate matter when explaining failure;
- immediate failure should still be separated from long-term legacy.

**Sources:** [The National Archives - What was Chartism?](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/what-was-chartism/) | [UK Parliament - 1842 and 1848 petitions](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/chartists/case-study/the-right-to-vote/the-chartists-and-birmingham/1842-and-1848-chartist-petitions/)

### H38 - Chartist legacy

By 1918, five of the Charter's six demands had effectively been achieved, although not because Parliament simply accepted Chartism in the 1840s.

**What this demonstrates:**

- immediate political failure and long-term significance are different judgements;
- later reforms cannot automatically be credited directly to Chartism without tracing the link.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Chartists and Birmingham](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/electionsvoting/chartists/case-study/the-right-to-vote/the-chartists-and-birmingham/)

# 7. Protest and campaigns in Wales and England

### H39 - Rebecca Riots, c.1839-1843

**Best fit:** Welsh rural protest, tollgates, economic grievance

Men disguised in women's clothing, described as the Daughters of Rebecca, attacked tollgates in west and mid Wales. Wider grievances included poverty, rents, tithes and resentment of local authority.

**What this demonstrates:**

- protest in Wales had distinctive rural causes as well as links to wider economic pressure;
- symbolic direct action could gain broad local support;
- reducing Rebecca only to tolls misses the wider context.

**Source:** [National Library of Wales - The Blue Books of 1847](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/printed-material/the-blue-books-of-1847)

### H40 - Rebecca archival evidence

The National Library of Wales holds correspondence, proclamations, reward notices and prisoner records connected with the Rebecca Riots.

**What this demonstrates:**

- one movement can be studied through state records, local correspondence and later accounts;
- provenance changes what each source is valuable for.

**Source:** [National Library of Wales Archives - Rebecca Riots](https://archives.library.wales/index.php/rebecca-riots)

### H41 - Anti-Corn Law League

**Best fit:** organised pressure, finance, print, political lobbying

The League used national organisation, meetings, petitions, printed propaganda and electoral pressure rather than the armed tactics associated with Newport or attacks on Rebecca tollgates.

**What this demonstrates:**

- movements differed in resources, social base and access to political influence;
- organisation and compatibility with elite interests can help explain different outcomes.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Petitions and the Corn Laws](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/326/petitions-committee/news/99040/petitions-and-the-co%20rn-laws/)

### H42 - Repeal of the Corn Laws, 1846

**Best fit:** Peel, League pressure, Irish Famine, political consequence

Peel's Conservative government secured repeal in 1846. Pressure from the Anti-Corn Law League combined with the Irish Famine and Peel's changing judgement, while repeal split the Conservative Party.

**Short primary quotation from Cobden:**

> "no power on earth could prevent" repeal

**What this demonstrates:**

- successful reform usually has several causes;
- campaign pressure matters, but it should be weighed against crisis and leadership inside government.

**Sources:** [UK Parliament - Petitions and the Corn Laws](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/326/petitions-committee/news/99040/petitions-and-the-co%20rn-laws/) | [Historic Hansard - Corn Law repeal debate](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1846/mar/03/total-and-immediate-repeal-of-the-corn)

### H43 - Comparing protest methods

Compare:

- Chartist petitions and mass meetings;
- Newport's armed confrontation;
- Rebecca attacks on tollgates;
- Anti-Corn Law League lobbying, organisation and electoral pressure.

**What this demonstrates:** Method cannot be evaluated separately from social base, resources, political opportunity and the aims being pursued.

# 8. Unit 4 Source Evaluation Bank

### H44 - War Office plan of the Westgate Hotel, 1839

The National Archives holds a War Office plan showing troop positions and bodies during the Newport Rising.

**Value:** strong for reconstructing official military positioning and how the confrontation was recorded by the state.

**Limitation:** it does not directly reveal the motives or experiences of ordinary Chartist marchers.

**Source:** [The National Archives - People's Charter podcast and records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/on-the-record-podcast/on-the-record-the-peoples-charter/)

### H45 - The 1842 Chartist petition

**Value:** excellent evidence for declared Chartist grievances, demands and the scale of petitioning organisation.

**Limitation:** signatures do not prove that every supporter shared identical motives or supported every tactic.

**Source:** [Historic Hansard - People's Charter petition](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/02/the-peoples-charter-petition)

### H46 - Mines Commission evidence, 1842

The Royal Commission's report publicised conditions for women and children underground and used vivid illustrations.

**Value:** important for what reformers and Parliament were being shown and why political pressure increased.

**Limitation:** investigators selected evidence for a reform enquiry, so representativeness and purpose should be considered.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Coal mines](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/livinglearning/19thcentury/overview/coalmines/)

### H47 - Rebecca proclamations and reward notices

**Value:** reveal how authorities defined the disturbances, what behaviour they feared and how they tried to identify participants.

**Limitation:** state language may frame protesters primarily as criminals and understate local grievances.

**Source:** [National Library of Wales Archives - Rebecca Riots](https://archives.library.wales/index.php/rebecca-riots)

### H48 - Cobden in parliamentary debate, 1846

**Value:** direct evidence of Anti-Corn Law campaigning strategy and Cobden's confidence in organised national pressure.

**Limitation:** as a leading campaigner speaking during a decisive political struggle, Cobden had a persuasive purpose and an interest in presenting the League as powerful and legitimate.

**Source:** [Historic Hansard - 3 March 1846](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1846/mar/03/total-and-immediate-repeal-of-the-corn)

## 9. Process examples only

### H49 - A primary archive record

A source such as a letter, government report, newspaper, court record, speech, map or photograph should be logged with creator, date, provenance, relevance and what it can actually establish.

**What this demonstrates:** Primary does not mean automatically true or unbiased. Value depends on the enquiry.

### H50 - A published historian's interpretation

Record the historian's **argument**, supporting evidence, scope, assumptions and how the interpretation compares with another historian. Do not collect quotations without understanding the argument around them.

**What this demonstrates:** Unit 5 evaluates interpretations, not simply historians' names.

### H51 - WJEC authentication and enquiry boundaries

WJEC requires a 3,000-4,000 word historical enquiry, approved through the required process, using all three assessment objectives. The enquiry must remain Kellyn's own work.

**What this demonstrates:** Kellyn Hub can support source organisation, planning, reminders and understanding of historical method, but must not generate the assessed argument or submission text.

**Source:** [WJEC GCE History specification](https://www.wjec.co.uk/media/uwlondvv/wjec-gce-history-spec-from-2015-e.pdf)

# Final exam-use reminders

- A famous example is not automatically the most significant example.
- Do not use one decade to answer a century-long Unit 3 question.
- A law being passed does not prove that implementation was complete.
- A protest movement can fail immediately but still be historically significant.
- Do not write that a source is "biased therefore useless".
- Provenance matters only when you explain how it changes the source's value for the enquiry.
- Use short quotations as evidence, not decoration.
- The strongest judgement compares examples directly and explains **why one matters more than another**.
