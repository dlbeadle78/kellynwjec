## How to use this resource

There are two different kinds of example in this document:

- **Legal authority** - a case, statute or official rule that helps establish what the law is.
- **Real-world illustration** - a recent case, statistic, policy or event that helps show what the law looks like in practice.

Do not treat a news report about a criminal case as if it creates the legal rule. Learn the rule from the statute or legal authority, then use the real-world example to make the rule easier to understand, remember, apply or evaluate.

For **Unit 3**, use examples to practise spotting issues and applying rules to facts.  
For **Unit 4**, ask what each example shows about fairness, clarity, effectiveness, consistency, rights, public protection and possible reform.

Law changes over time. Current examples are dated so they can be checked against later developments and teacher guidance.

# Part A - Criminal Law

## 1. The criminal justice process, proof and prosecution

### Example A1 - The CPS Full Code Test

**Topic:** CPS, prosecution decisions, burden and standard of proof

The Crown Prosecution Service does not prosecute simply because an allegation has been made. Under the Full Code Test, prosecutors first ask whether there is sufficient evidence to provide a **realistic prospect of conviction**. If that stage is passed, they consider whether a prosecution is required in the **public interest**.

The CPS also makes clear that its prosecution test is different from the test used by a criminal court. A court may convict only if it is **sure that the defendant is guilty**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the difference between the decision to prosecute and the decision to convict;
- why evidential sufficiency matters before a case reaches trial;
- how public interest forms part of prosecution decision-making;
- why the prosecution carries the criminal burden of proof.

**Unit 3 link:** In a scenario, separate "there is enough evidence to charge/prosecute" from "the prosecution can prove the offence at trial".

**Unit 4 link:** Consider whether the Full Code Test provides an effective safeguard against weak or unnecessary prosecutions.

**Source:** [CPS - The Code for Crown Prosecutors](https://www.cps.gov.uk/publication/code-crown-prosecutors)

### Example A2 - Remand in custody in England and Wales

**Topic:** bail, remand and the criminal process

Ministry of Justice statistics for the year ending March 2026 show that around **67,000 defendants were remanded in custody at magistrates' courts**, the highest volume in the last decade, although this represented about 5% of defendants. At the Crown Court, **54% of defendants were remanded in custody**, similar to the previous year.

**What this demonstrates:**

- remand is a real and significant part of the criminal justice process;
- more serious cases are more likely to involve custody before trial;
- bail and remand decisions involve balancing liberty, risk, attendance at court and public protection.

**Unit 4 thinking:** A person remanded in custody has not necessarily been convicted. That creates an obvious tension between the presumption of innocence and the need to manage genuine risks.

**Source:** [Ministry of Justice - Criminal Justice Statistics quarterly: March 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/criminal-justice-statistics-quarterly-march-2026/criminal-justice-statistics-quarterly-march-2026-html)

### Example A3 - Youth remand

**Topic:** youth courts, remand and fairness

Youth Justice Board statistics for the year ending March 2025 recorded around **11,100 remand decisions for children**. About 78% were bail remands, 13% were community remands with intervention and 9% involved youth detention accommodation. Almost two-thirds of children who had been remanded to youth detention accommodation did not later receive a custodial sentence.

**What this demonstrates:**

- children are treated through a distinct youth justice framework;
- remand does not predict the final sentence;
- the use of custody before trial can be evaluated in terms of necessity, proportionality and fairness.

**Source:** [Youth Justice Board - Youth Justice Statistics 2024 to 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025)

## 2. Actus reus, mens rea and causation

### Example A4 - Cardiff murder and evidence of intention

**Topic:** mens rea, intention, murder

In February 2026, Thisara Weragalage was sentenced for murdering his wife in Cardiff. The CPS described evidence that he had visited the area beforehand, knew where she parked and had bought knives the previous day. He pleaded guilty to murder.

**What this demonstrates:**

- intention is normally inferred from evidence and circumstances rather than read directly from a person's mind;
- planning, preparation, weapon choice and conduct before an offence can all be relevant evidence;
- a real case can contain several facts from which mens rea may be inferred.

**Important:** The CPS report is an illustration of evidential reasoning. The legal definition of intention still comes from the relevant legal authorities.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Man sentenced to life imprisonment for murdering his wife, 20 February 2026](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/man-sentenced-life-imprisonment-murdering-his-wife)

### Example A5 - R v Hughes and meaningful causation

**Topic:** legal causation

In **R v Hughes [2013] UKSC 56**, an uninsured and unlicensed driver was involved in a fatal collision, but the evidence did not show that his driving had contributed to the accident in the necessary way. The Supreme Court rejected an approach that would make a defendant criminally responsible for a death merely because he was driving unlawfully when the collision occurred.

**What this demonstrates:**

- being present in the chain of events is not automatically enough;
- criminal liability for a result requires a legally meaningful causal connection;
- causation protects against attaching a serious result to conduct that did not contribute to it in the required way.

**Source:** [UK Supreme Court - R v Hughes](https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2011-0240)

### Example A6 - One punch, two different homicide outcomes

**Topic:** causation, murder, manslaughter, mens rea

Two recent South Wales cases are useful to compare carefully.

- **Christopher Cooper** punched Kelvin Evans once outside a pub in Gorseinon. Mr Evans died around a month later. Cooper had offered a guilty plea to manslaughter, but the jury convicted him of murder after hearing evidence about intention.
- **Jason Thomas** punched Liam Morgan-Whittle twice in Llanelli. Mr Morgan-Whittle died from his injuries. Thomas pleaded guilty to manslaughter.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the number of blows does not determine whether an offence is murder or manslaughter;
- the prosecution must establish the required mens rea for the particular homicide offence;
- a later death can still be legally connected to the original attack where causation is established;
- apparently similar factual events can produce different legal outcomes because the evidence and mental element differ.

**Sources:**

- [CPS Cymru-Wales - Man sentenced for one punch pub murder, 3 January 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/man-sentenced-one-punch-pub-murder)
- [CPS Cymru-Wales - Llanelli man sentenced for unlawful killing, 3 March 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/llanelli-man-sentenced-unlawful-killing)

### Example A7 - Strict liability and public protection

**Topic:** strict liability and the Gammon approach

**Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v Attorney-General of Hong Kong [1985] AC 1** is the recognised authority used to explain when a statutory offence may impose strict liability. The case involved building work that deviated from approved plans and created public-safety concerns.

The wider idea is still relevant to modern regulatory law: Parliament may sometimes prioritise public safety, effective regulation and greater vigilance over requiring the prosecution to prove fault for every element of an offence.

**What this demonstrates:**

- criminal law normally presumes a mental element is required;
- strict liability is an exception and needs justification;
- Unit 4 evaluation should consider both enforcement effectiveness and fairness to a defendant who may not be morally blameworthy.

**Supporting authority:** [BAILII - Royal Mail Group Ltd v Watson [2021] EWHC 2098 (Admin), discussing Gammon and strict liability](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2021/2098.html)

## 3. Murder, manslaughter and partial defences

### Example A8 - Dylan Thomas and diminished responsibility

**Topic:** diminished responsibility

Dylan Thomas killed his housemate William Bush in Cardiff on Christmas Eve 2023. At trial he admitted manslaughter on the basis of diminished responsibility but denied murder. Psychiatric evidence was heard. The jury rejected the partial defence and convicted him of murder.

The CPS also relied on evidence of his actions before the killing, including internet searches about neck anatomy and arming himself with knives.

**What this demonstrates:**

- a psychiatric diagnosis does not automatically establish diminished responsibility;
- the statutory requirements still have to be proved;
- expert evidence is important, but the ultimate legal decision is for the court/jury;
- conduct before the killing may be relevant to the assessment of responsibility and intention.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Man who murdered his housemate on Christmas Eve sentenced, 24 January 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/man-who-murdered-his-housemate-christmas-eve-sentenced)

### Example A9 - R v Golds and "substantial" impairment

**Topic:** diminished responsibility, authority

In **R v Golds [2016] UKSC 61**, the Supreme Court considered the meaning of "substantially impaired" in the diminished responsibility defence. The Court emphasised that more than a merely trivial impairment is required. Whether the impairment is substantial is a question of degree for the jury.

**What this demonstrates:**

- legal tests often contain evaluative language rather than mathematical thresholds;
- medical evidence and the legal test are not the same thing;
- the partial defence substantially changes the conviction from murder to manslaughter, so the threshold matters.

**Source:** [UK Supreme Court - R v Golds](https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2015-0053)

### Example A10 - Intoxication does not automatically create diminished responsibility

**Topic:** intoxication and diminished responsibility

Dean Mears was convicted of murder after killing Catherine Flynn in Rhyl. At trial he relied on diminished responsibility. Evidence showed ketamine, cannabis and cocaine in his system, alongside psychiatric evidence. The jury rejected the partial defence.

**What this demonstrates:**

- intoxication and a recognised medical condition must not be confused;
- voluntary intoxication does not automatically excuse or reduce criminal responsibility;
- where intoxication and mental abnormality are both present, the legal analysis becomes more careful, not less.

**Legal guidance:** CPS guidance explains that voluntary acute intoxication cannot itself establish diminished responsibility. Where a recognised condition also exists, its effect must be assessed separately from the voluntary intoxication.

**Sources:**

- [CPS Cymru-Wales - Life imprisonment for man who killed woman in her own home, 20 June 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/life-imprisonment-man-who-killed-woman-her-own-home)
- [CPS - Mental Health: Suspects and Defendants](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/mental-health-suspects-and-defendants)

### Example A11 - Homicide law is under active review in 2026

**Topic:** Unit 4 evaluation and reform

The Law Commission opened a major homicide consultation on **17 June 2026**, due to close on **30 September 2026**. Its provisional proposals include replacing the current murder/manslaughter structure with a three-tier structure involving first-degree murder, second-degree murder and manslaughter, with a separate way of recognising murder where a partial defence succeeds.

**What this demonstrates:**

- criticism of homicide law is not only academic: reform is actively being considered;
- reform debates focus on culpability, labels, fairness and proportionality of sentencing;
- current murder and manslaughter categories can be evaluated against whether they accurately reflect different levels of blameworthiness.

**Unit 4 question to ask:** Would a graded homicide structure produce fairer and clearer outcomes, or would it make the law more complex?

**Source:** [Law Commission - Law of homicide](https://lawcom.gov.uk/project/law-of-homicide/)

## 4. Non-fatal offences against the person

### Example A12 - Serious injury and GBH with intent in Cwmbrân

**Topic:** grievous bodily harm and intention

Richard Nodwell attacked a Gwent Police officer in Cwmbrân, repeatedly punching and kicking him. The officer suffered a fractured cheekbone, fractured eye socket, fractured nose and other wounds. Nodwell pleaded guilty to causing GBH with intent.

**What this demonstrates:**

- "grievous bodily harm" means really serious harm and can be understood through the severity of actual injuries;
- section 18 requires the more serious mental element, so the facts supporting intention matter as well as the injuries;
- applying an offence means dealing separately with actus reus and mens rea.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Man jailed for brutal assault on Gwent Police officer, 1 April 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/man-jailed-brutal-assault-gwent-police-officer)

### Example A13 - Attempted GBH where the final injury was less serious

**Topic:** attempts and offences against the person

In April 2025, a Swansea headteacher was jailed after repeatedly striking his deputy with a metal wrench. The victim required hospital treatment, but the CPS described evidence showing an intention to cause really serious harm. The defendant pleaded guilty to **attempted GBH with intent**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- attempts focus on what the defendant intended and how far they went towards the offence, not only the injury that happened;
- a defendant may intend a more serious result than actually occurs;
- this is a useful bridge between the non-fatal offences topic and attempts.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Headteacher jailed for attacking his deputy, 25 April 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/headteacher-jailed-attacking-his-deputy)

### Example A14 - Why reform of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 is discussed

**Topic:** Unit 4 reform

The Law Commission has criticised the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 because offences are not clearly ordered by seriousness, some mental elements are unclear, terminology is archaic and the overall scheme has become incoherent after repeated amendment and repeal.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the main statute for serious non-fatal offences dates from the nineteenth century;
- case law has had to interpret old wording for modern situations;
- age alone does not prove a law is bad, but unclear structure and terminology can affect accessibility and consistency.

**Source:** [Law Commission - Offences against the person](https://lawcom.gov.uk/project/offences-against-the-person/)

## 5. Theft, robbery and burglary

### Example A15 - Swansea taxi robbery

**Topic:** robbery

In 2025, Nicholas Daniel threatened a Swansea taxi driver with scissors, told him to leave the vehicle and took the car. He pleaded guilty to robbery.

**What this demonstrates:**

- robbery is not simply "taking something";
- it combines theft with force or threat of force used in connection with stealing;
- the facts should be split into the theft elements and the force/threat element.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Carjacker sentenced for robbing Swansea taxi driver, 2 October 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/carjacker-sentenced-robbing-swansea-taxi-driver)

### Example A16 - Anglesey cattle burglary and modern evidence

**Topic:** burglary, property, criminal evidence in practice

Five men were sentenced in March 2026 after conspiring to burgle cattle from an Anglesey farm. Fourteen cattle had gone missing. Police pieced the case together using CCTV, phone messages, cell-site information and DNA testing of recovered cattle.

**What this demonstrates:**

- burglary applies well beyond the stereotype of breaking into a house to steal televisions;
- property offences can involve rural businesses, livestock and organised offending;
- modern prosecutions may combine many forms of digital, scientific and circumstantial evidence.

**Important:** Use the Theft Act rules to decide the exact legal elements. The news story illustrates a prosecution, not the statutory definition by itself.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Organised cattle theft on Anglesey, 20 March 2026](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/five-men-including-farmer-sentenced-after-dna-tests-exposed-organised-cattle-theft)

## 6. Insanity, automatism, intoxication, self-defence and duress

### Example A17 - The insanity rules date from 1843

**Topic:** insanity and automatism, Unit 4 reform

The Law Commission states that the current insanity rules date from **1843** and have been criticised because the legal test can lag behind modern psychiatric understanding, the label "insane" is outdated, the defence is underused and the distinction between insane and non-insane automatism can produce incoherent results.

A new Law Commission project forms part of its 14th Programme of Law Reform, although substantive work had not yet commenced when this document was reviewed.

**What this demonstrates:**

- legal insanity is a technical legal concept, not the same as a medical diagnosis;
- the internal/external cause distinction has major consequences for the outcome;
- this is a strong Unit 4 example of a law that can be criticised for terminology, coherence and fit with modern knowledge.

**Source:** [Law Commission - The defence of insanity](https://lawcom.gov.uk/project/the-defence-of-insanity/)

### Example A18 - Automatism: internal and external causes

**Topic:** automatism

Current CPS guidance gives practical examples of the distinction:

- epilepsy, hyperglycaemia and some sleepwalking cases may fall within "insane automatism" because the cause is treated as internal;
- concussion from a blow to the head or an insulin injection may support non-insane automatism where the legal requirements are met.

The guidance stresses that automatism requires a loss of conscious control, not simply reduced control.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the legal classification depends heavily on the cause of the condition;
- similar-looking involuntary behaviour can lead to different legal outcomes;
- this provides material for evaluating whether the current distinction is fair or scientifically convincing.

**Source:** [CPS - Mental Health: Suspects and Defendants](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/mental-health-suspects-and-defendants)

### Example A19 - Self-defence has a subjective and an objective stage

**Topic:** self-defence

CPS guidance summarises self-defence through two main questions:

1. Did the defendant genuinely believe force was necessary in the circumstances as they understood them?
2. If so, was the force used reasonable?

A mistaken belief caused by voluntary intoxication cannot normally be relied on to justify self-defence.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the law considers both the defendant's perception and the reasonableness of the force;
- self-defence is not simply "I felt threatened";
- the facts need to be analysed in a sequence.

**Source:** [CPS - Mental Health: Suspects and Defendants](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/mental-health-suspects-and-defendants)

### Example A20 - Duress is not available for murder or attempted murder

**Topic:** duress

CPS guidance confirms that common-law duress is unavailable for murder and attempted murder. This creates a clear boundary even where a defendant claims to have acted under threats.

**What this demonstrates:**

- defences reflect policy as well as individual fault;
- the law sometimes refuses a defence because of the seriousness of the offence and the value placed on innocent life;
- Unit 4 can evaluate whether an absolute exclusion is fair in extreme cases.

**Source:** [CPS - Mental Health: Suspects and Defendants](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/mental-health-suspects-and-defendants)

## 7. Attempts

### Example A21 - Ysgol Dyffryn Aman attempted murders

**Topic:** attempted murder, intention, youth justice

A 14-year-old girl was convicted in February 2025 of attempting to murder two teachers and a pupil at Ysgol Dyffryn Aman in Carmarthenshire. She had admitted carrying out the stabbings but denied attempted murder. Evidence included repeated stabbing and statements that she was going to kill. She was 13 when the incident took place.

**What this demonstrates:**

- attempted murder requires proof of an intention to kill, which is narrower than the mens rea for murder;
- the full offence need not be completed for attempt liability;
- words, weapon use, repeated conduct and surrounding facts can provide evidence of intention;
- youth justice rules can operate alongside serious substantive offences.

**Source:** [CPS Cymru-Wales - Teenage girl convicted of three attempted murders, 3 February 2025](https://www.cps.gov.uk/cymruwales/news/teenage-girl-convicted-three-attempted-murders)

### Example A22 - Impossible attempts can still be criminal

**Topic:** Criminal Attempts Act 1981

Section 1 of the Criminal Attempts Act 1981 states that a person may be guilty of attempt even where the full offence is impossible in the actual circumstances, provided the statutory requirements are met.

**Simple illustration:** A person reaches into a pocket intending to steal a phone, but the pocket is empty. Factual impossibility does not automatically prevent attempt liability.

**What this demonstrates:**

- criminal attempt focuses strongly on intention plus conduct that has gone beyond mere preparation;
- the law can punish dangerous criminal purpose even when chance makes completion impossible.

**Source:** [Criminal Attempts Act 1981, section 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/47/section/1)

# Part B - Human Rights Law

## 8. The Human Rights Act 1998 and the ECHR framework

### Example B1 - Section 6 HRA: public authorities must act compatibly with Convention rights

**Topic:** Human Rights Act 1998, public authorities

Section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998 makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with a Convention right, subject to the statutory exceptions.

**Real-world importance:** This is why decisions by police forces, councils, government departments and other public bodies can be challenged on human-rights grounds.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the HRA does not simply list abstract rights;
- it affects everyday public decision-making;
- human-rights questions often ask whether interference is lawful, necessary and proportionate rather than whether a right exists at all.

**Source:** [Human Rights Act 1998, section 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/section/6)

### Example B2 - The proposed Bill of Rights did not replace the HRA

**Topic:** reform of UK human-rights protection

A Bill of Rights Bill was introduced in 2022 but **withdrawn on 27 June 2023**. The Human Rights Act therefore remained in force when this resource was reviewed in September 2026.

**What this demonstrates:**

- proposals for constitutional change do not become law simply because they are announced or introduced;
- Unit 4 evaluation should distinguish the current law from political proposals;
- the debate over the future form of UK human-rights protection remains useful, but answers must be legally up to date.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Bill of Rights Bill](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/3227)

### Example B3 - A 2026 HRA remedial order in action

**Topic:** enforcement, declarations/incompatibility, reform mechanisms

In 2026 the Government laid a draft Human Rights Act remedial order to amend section 9 of the HRA following the European Court of Human Rights judgment in **SW v UK**. The proposal concerned access to damages and an incompatibility with Article 13.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the relationship between Strasbourg judgments, Parliament and domestic legislation is an active process;
- the HRA contains mechanisms for responding to identified incompatibilities without courts simply striking down Acts of Parliament;
- this is a current example of human-rights law influencing legislative change.

**Source:** [Ministry of Justice - Draft Human Rights Act 1998 (Remedial) Order 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/draft-human-rights-act-1998-remedial-order-2026)

## 9. Article 8 - privacy, family life, home and correspondence

### Example B4 - Bridges v South Wales Police: facial recognition in Cardiff

**Topic:** Article 8, police powers, surveillance, judicial review

In **R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police [2020] EWCA Civ 1058**, the Court of Appeal considered South Wales Police's use of live automated facial recognition. The technology captured faces of members of the public and compared them with a police watchlist.

The case established that this kind of deployment engages privacy rights and that the legal framework, safeguards and exercise of police discretion matter when deciding whether interference is lawful.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Article 8 applies to modern biometric technology, not only traditional searches of homes or correspondence;
- proportionality, safeguards and legal certainty are central to human-rights analysis;
- judicial review can be used to challenge the lawfulness of police policy.

**Source:** [Courts and Tribunals Judiciary - R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police](https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/r-bridges-v-cc-south-wales/)

### Example B5 - Facial recognition in South Wales after Bridges

**Topic:** privacy versus public protection, current context

Police use of facial recognition has continued and expanded rather than disappearing after Bridges. Home Office guidance published in December 2025 states that South Wales Police uses live and retrospective facial recognition, and that South Wales Police and Gwent Police use operator-initiated facial recognition.

Examples given by the Home Office include using the technology to locate wanted suspects, identify a missing child and identify vulnerable people who could not provide their details.

In August 2025 the Information Commissioner's Office published an audit of South Wales Police and Gwent Police. It reported a high level of assurance about the processes and safeguards it examined, while stressing that facial recognition carries risks including intrusion, discrimination and misidentification and must be lawful, fair and proportionate.

**What this demonstrates:**

- a court finding that a particular framework was unlawful does not mean a technology can never lawfully be used;
- legal safeguards can change in response to judgments;
- this gives a strong, local Unit 4 debate: privacy and equality risks versus crime prevention and safeguarding benefits.

**Sources:**

- [Home Office - Police use of facial recognition: factsheet, December 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/police-use-of-facial-recognition/police-use-of-facial-recognition-factsheet)
- [ICO - Audit of South Wales Police and Gwent Police, August 2025](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/08/ico-steps-up-scrutiny-of-police-use-of-facial-recognition-technology-with-audit-of-south-wales-police-and-gwent-police/)

### Example B6 - ZXC v Bloomberg: privacy before charge

**Topic:** Article 8, privacy, media, criminal investigations

In **Bloomberg LP v ZXC [2022] UKSC 5**, the Supreme Court considered whether a person who had not been charged could have a reasonable expectation of privacy regarding information about a criminal investigation.

**What this demonstrates:**

- being investigated is not the same as being convicted or even charged;
- Article 8 privacy and Article 10 freedom of expression can directly conflict;
- courts may have to balance the subject's reputation/privacy against the public and media interest in reporting.

**Source:** [UK Supreme Court - Bloomberg LP v ZXC](https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2020-0122)

## 10. Article 10 - freedom of expression

### Example B7 - Miller v College of Policing and the chilling effect

**Topic:** Article 10, police records, lawful speech

In **Miller v College of Policing [2021] EWCA Civ 1926**, the Court of Appeal considered police guidance on recording non-crime hate incidents following comments made on Twitter/X. The case is important when studying the idea of a **chilling effect**, where official action may discourage people from exercising lawful freedom of expression.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Article 10 protects controversial and unpopular expression within legal limits;
- even non-criminal police action may interfere with expression;
- proportionality matters when public authorities respond to speech that is offensive but not necessarily criminal.

**Source:** [Courts and Tribunals Judiciary - Miller v College of Policing](https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/miller-v-the-college-of-policing/)

### Example B8 - Defamation and serious harm: Lachaux

**Topic:** expression versus reputation, defamation

In **Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd [2019] UKSC 27**, the Supreme Court examined the "serious harm" requirement in section 1 of the Defamation Act 2013. A claimant must establish that the publication has caused or is likely to cause serious harm to reputation.

**What this demonstrates:**

- defamation law balances protection of reputation against freedom of expression;
- Parliament raised the threshold so that trivial reputational harm does not automatically justify a defamation action;
- the effect of publication in the real world matters, not only whether words sound damaging.

**Source:** [UK Supreme Court - Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd](https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2017-0175)

## 11. Article 11 - assembly and association, protest and public order

### Example B9 - DPP v Ziegler: obstructive protest can still require a rights analysis

**Topic:** Articles 10 and 11, protest, proportionality

In **DPP v Ziegler [2021] UKSC 23**, protesters had obstructed a highway during a demonstration against an arms fair. The case confirmed the importance of considering Articles 10 and 11 and proportionality when deciding whether protest-related conduct is criminal.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Convention rights can apply even where protest causes physical obstruction;
- the existence of a criminal offence does not always remove the need for a proportionality analysis;
- peaceful protest rights are qualified, not unlimited.

**Source:** [BAILII - DPP v Ziegler [2021] UKSC 23](https://www.bailii.org/uk/cases/UKSC/2021/23.html)

### Example B10 - Public Order Act 2023 and disruptive protest

**Topic:** public order restrictions, Articles 10 and 11

The Public Order Act 2023 introduced new offences and powers aimed at highly disruptive protest tactics, including the offence of **locking on**. The legislation also develops the concept of "serious disruption" and sits alongside existing powers to impose conditions on processions and assemblies.

**What this demonstrates:**

- protest law changes as new forms of protest develop;
- Parliament can restrict the manner of protest to protect other people's rights, public safety and essential activities;
- Unit 4 evaluation can ask whether the rules strike a fair balance or deter legitimate protest.

**Sources:**

- [Public Order Act 2023 - policy background](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/15/notes/division/3/index.htm)
- [Public Order Act 2023 - locking on](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/15/notes/division/6/index.htm)

## 12. Police powers, stop and search and searches of premises

### Example B11 - Stop and search requires legal grounds

**Topic:** police powers, liberty, privacy

Current PACE Code A says stop and search powers must be used fairly, responsibly and without unlawful discrimination. Where reasonable suspicion is required, an officer needs both a genuine suspicion and an objective basis for it. A hunch that cannot be explained is not enough.

**What this demonstrates:**

- police powers are legally controlled rather than unlimited;
- individual liberty and crime prevention have to be balanced;
- safeguards such as reasons, recording and objective grounds are part of legality and accountability.

**Sources:**

- [GOV.UK - Police powers to stop and search: your rights](https://www.gov.uk/police-powers-to-stop-and-search-your-rights/police-powers-stop-search)
- [Home Office - PACE Code A 2023, accessible version](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pace-code-a-2023/pace-code-a-2023-accessible)

### Example B12 - Searching premises and Article 8

**Topic:** Article 8, police entry/search/seizure

PACE Code B explicitly recognises privacy and respect for personal property as important Human Rights Act principles. It says entry, search and seizure powers should be clearly justified because of their potential intrusion, and officers should consider whether less intrusive means can achieve the objective.

**What this demonstrates:**

- human-rights reasoning is built into operational policing guidance;
- "necessary and proportionate" is not just an exam phrase: it affects how real powers should be used;
- the same framework can be applied to hypothetical Unit 3 scenarios involving searches of homes and property.

**Source:** [Home Office - PACE Code B 2023, accessible version](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pace-code-b-2023/pace-code-b-2023-accessible)

## 13. Surveillance and interception

### Example B13 - Big Brother Watch v UK

**Topic:** surveillance, interception, Articles 8 and 10, ECtHR

In **Big Brother Watch and Others v United Kingdom [2021]**, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights examined UK bulk interception and communications-data regimes. It found violations of Article 8 in aspects of the surveillance framework and Article 10 in relation to insufficient safeguards for confidential journalistic material.

**What this demonstrates:**

- national security does not remove human-rights safeguards;
- large-scale digital surveillance raises questions about authorisation, oversight, necessity and protection of confidential material;
- Article 8 privacy and Article 10 press freedom can overlap.

**Source:** [European Court of Human Rights - Big Brother Watch and Others v UK](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-210077)

## 14. Confidentiality, harassment and obscenity

### Example B14 - Harassment requires a course of conduct

**Topic:** harassment

The Protection from Harassment Act 1997 provides that a "course of conduct" involves conduct on at least two occasions, and harassment includes alarming a person or causing distress. Conduct can include speech.

**Real-world illustration:** repeated unwanted messages, repeated following or repeated contact may be legally different from a single unpleasant interaction because the statute focuses on a course of conduct.

**What this demonstrates:**

- everyday words such as "harassment" may have more precise legal requirements;
- frequency, context and the effect of conduct matter;
- online and offline behaviour can both potentially form part of a course of conduct.

**Source:** [Protection from Harassment Act 1997](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1997/40/contents)

### Example B15 - The legal test for obscenity comes from a 1959 statute

**Topic:** obscenity, expression and morality

The Obscene Publications Act 1959 defines an article as obscene where its effect, taken as a whole, is such as to tend to "deprave and corrupt" people likely to encounter it.

**What this demonstrates:**

- obscenity law involves the relationship between law, morality and freedom of expression;
- older statutory language has to operate in a world of digital media and online publication that Parliament in 1959 could not have anticipated;
- Unit 4 can evaluate whether the wording remains clear and workable for modern society.

**Source:** [Obscene Publications Act 1959](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1959/66/contents)

## 15. Enforcement, judicial review and the European Court of Human Rights

### Example B16 - Bridges as a judicial review example

**Topic:** judicial review

Bridges did not begin as a criminal prosecution. It was a challenge to the lawfulness of a public body's policy and use of power.

**What this demonstrates:**

- judicial review examines whether a public authority has acted lawfully;
- human-rights arguments can be raised through public law procedures;
- a claimant can challenge the framework governing a public authority's actions, not only seek compensation after harm.

**Source:** [Courts and Tribunals Judiciary - R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police](https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/r-bridges-v-cc-south-wales/)

### Example B17 - Hirst and the relationship with Strasbourg

**Topic:** European Court of Human Rights, enforcement, sovereignty debate

In **Hirst v United Kingdom (No. 2)**, the European Court of Human Rights held that the UK's automatic blanket ban on voting by convicted prisoners breached the right to free elections under Article 3 of Protocol No. 1.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the ECtHR can find that UK law or practice breaches Convention rights;
- implementation may involve political and constitutional disagreement rather than an instant change to domestic legislation;
- the case is useful when evaluating the relationship between Strasbourg, Parliament and democratic decision-making.

**Source:** [European Court of Human Rights - Prisoners' right to vote factsheet](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/fs_prisoners_vote_eng.pdf)

### Example B18 - What the Equality and Human Rights Commission can actually do

**Topic:** EHRC role and enforcement

The Equality and Human Rights Commission has statutory litigation and enforcement powers. It can bring some legal proceedings in its own name, intervene in cases and seek judicial review where a public body may have breached equality or human-rights law. Its human-rights mandate covers England and Wales.

In 2026 the EHRC intervened in a judicial review concerning the Metropolitan Police's live facial recognition policy and compatibility with Articles 8, 10 and 11, showing that its role is active rather than purely advisory.

**What this demonstrates:**

- the EHRC can help shape human-rights law through litigation and intervention;
- enforcement of rights involves institutions as well as individual court claims;
- facial recognition remains a live legal issue involving privacy, speech and assembly.

**Sources:**

- [EHRC - Litigation powers](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/our-work/our-legal-work/our-legal-powers/our-litigation-and-enforcement-policy/our-litigation-powers)
- [EHRC - Thompson v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis intervention, 13 March 2026](https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/our-work/king-application-thompson-and-another-v-commissioner-police-metropolis-summary-our)
