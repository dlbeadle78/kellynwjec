# WJEC A Level Sociology - Year 2 Real-World Examples, Evidence and Quotes Bank

**Kellyn's Year 2 route:** Unit 3 Crime and Deviance; Unit 4 Social Inequality and Applied Methods of Sociological Enquiry  
**Last reviewed:** 7 September 2026

This resource sits alongside the Year 2 Sociology Study Guide. It gives named studies, short sociological quotations, current official statistics, Welsh examples and real-world cases that can be used to understand and evaluate the ideas in the course.

## Why this matters

WJEC examiner feedback repeatedly stresses that higher-level Sociology needs **specific sociological evidence**, not vague phrases such as "statistics show" and not unsupported anecdotes. Current evidence should therefore be used with named sociologists and theories rather than replacing them.

Use three labels carefully:

- **Sociological evidence**: a named study, sociologist, theory or established research finding.
- **Current evidence**: official statistics or contemporary research showing a present-day pattern.
- **Real-world illustration**: an event, policy or situation that makes an idea easier to see, but does not by itself prove a sociological theory.

For Unit 3, ask: **What theory or explanation does this evidence support, challenge or complicate?**  
For Unit 4, ask: **What pattern of inequality does this show, and which theory explains it best?**  
For Applied Methods, ask: **What does the research design allow the researcher to know, and what are its limits?**

Current statistics are dated because they will change. Always prefer the most recent release available when revising close to the examination.

# Part A - Unit 3: Crime and Deviance

## 1. Crime, deviance and social construction

### Example S1 - Legal definitions change: sexuality and the Sexual Offences Act 1967

**Best fit:** Social construction of crime and deviance

The Sexual Offences Act 1967 partially decriminalised private consensual sexual acts between men over 21 in England and Wales. Later legal changes reduced the age threshold and expanded equality protections.

**What this demonstrates:**

- conduct can move between criminal, deviant, tolerated and accepted categories;
- legal definitions are historically and socially located rather than timeless;
- changes in law can both reflect and influence wider social attitudes.

**Use carefully:** This does not mean all crime is arbitrary. It shows that the legal boundary itself can change.

**Source:** [UK Parliament - Sexual Offences Act 1967](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1967/60/contents)

### Example S2 - Becker and labelling

**Best fit:** Labelling theory, social reaction

Howard Becker argues that deviance is produced partly through rule-making and rule-enforcement. A useful short quotation is:

> "The deviant is one to whom that label has been successfully applied."

**What this demonstrates:**

- interactionism shifts attention from the act alone to the social reaction to it;
- being officially labelled may change identity, opportunities and later treatment;
- different people committing similar acts may not have the same chance of becoming officially defined as deviant.

**Source:** [OpenLearn - Becker and labelling theory](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=19462&printable=1)

### Example S3 - The Edinburgh Study: formal intervention can matter

**Best fit:** Labelling, secondary deviance, criminalisation

The Edinburgh Study of Youth Transitions and Crime followed a cohort of more than 4,000 young people and combined self-report surveys with official records. Researchers found that early formal justice intervention could stigmatise young people and that conviction patterns did not simply mirror self-reported offending.

**What this demonstrates:**

- official processing can become part of the explanation rather than merely recording offending;
- labelling theory has empirical support when examining criminal justice contact;
- self-reported behaviour and official criminal records can tell different stories.

**Source:** [University of Edinburgh - Edinburgh Study of Youth Transitions and Crime](https://www.law.ed.ac.uk/research/impact-and-engagement/edinburgh-study-of-youth-transitions-and-crime)

### Example S4 - Cohen, Mods and Rockers and moral panic

**Best fit:** Media, folk devils, moral panic

Stanley Cohen studied media and public reaction to clashes involving Mods and Rockers at English seaside towns in the 1960s. He argued that media and authority responses could magnify a perceived threat.

A useful short quotation is:

> "a condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests"

**What this demonstrates:**

- media representation can be part of the social reaction to deviance;
- folk devils can become symbols onto which wider fears are projected;
- increased reporting, policing and political reaction can amplify an issue.

**Use carefully:** Moral-panic theory does not prove that every strongly reported social problem is exaggerated.

**Source:** [OpenLearn - Media and moral panics](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/society-politics-law/discovering-disorder-young-people-and-delinquency/content-section-3.4)

## 2. Measuring crime and patterns of offending

### Example S5 - Crime Survey for England and Wales versus police-recorded crime

**Best fit:** Official statistics, victim surveys, hidden figure

The ONS Crime in England and Wales release for the year ending March 2026 brings together the Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW) and police-recorded crime. They measure different things. The CSEW asks people about experiences whether or not they reached the police, while police data record offences that became known to and were recorded by the police.

**What this demonstrates:**

- there is no single complete count of all crime;
- a trend can differ between datasets because the measures are different;
- source and method must be identified before interpreting a statistic.

**Source:** [ONS - Crime in England and Wales: year ending March 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/bulletins/crimeinenglandandwales/latest)

### Example S6 - Shoplifting statistics and the reporting problem

**Best fit:** Police-recorded crime, validity of official statistics

Police recorded **507,086 shoplifting offences** in the year ending March 2026, 4% fewer than the previous year. ONS explicitly warns that recent changes reflect both offending and improved reporting by retailers following closer work with police.

**What this demonstrates:**

- a recorded-crime trend can be affected by reporting and recording behaviour;
- an increase or decrease in a police statistic is not automatically an equivalent change in underlying offending;
- sociologists should interpret the production of statistics as well as the final number.

**Source:** [ONS - Crime in England and Wales: year ending March 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/bulletins/crimeinenglandandwales/latest)

### Example S7 - The Cardiff Model and the hidden figure of violence

**Best fit:** Hidden crime, triangulation, victim reporting

Research underpinning the Cardiff Model compared emergency-department information about violent injury with police records. Early work found that only **23%** of violent incidents seen in emergency departments appeared in police records. Cardiff then developed routine anonymised sharing of violence-location data between health services, police and local government.

**What this demonstrates:**

- serious incidents can remain outside police statistics;
- combining independent sources can reveal a larger hidden figure;
- data collection is not neutral: what agencies know depends partly on whether victims report.

**Source:** [Cardiff University - The Cardiff Model for Violence Prevention](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/documents/2665796-the-cardiff-model-for-violence-prevention)

### Example S8 - Self-report evidence from the Edinburgh Study

**Best fit:** Self-report studies

The Edinburgh Study repeatedly asked young people about their own offending and combined this with official agency data. The researchers found self-reports gave a much fuller account of delinquency than official records. Later work also found that conviction patterns did not necessarily match patterns of self-reported offending.

**What this demonstrates:**

- self-report studies can uncover offending that never reaches official statistics;
- they can challenge stereotypes based only on recorded crime;
- self-reports still depend on honesty, memory and willingness to disclose.

**Source:** [University of Edinburgh - About the Edinburgh Study](https://blogs.ed.ac.uk/edinstudy/about-the-study/)

## 3. Sociological theories and explanations of crime

### Example S9 - Durkheim: crime as normal

**Best fit:** Functionalism

Durkheim's argument is often summarised through his deliberately challenging claim that **"crime is normal"**. He did not mean crime is morally good. He argued that some deviance is inevitable in a society and may contribute to boundary maintenance and social change.

**What this demonstrates:**

- functionalism asks what social role deviance can play, not only why individuals offend;
- social reaction can reinforce shared norms;
- too much or too little regulation can be sociologically significant.

**Source:** [Sociology Compass - discussion of Durkheim's crime-as-normal argument](https://doi.org/10.1111/soc4.70205)

### Example S10 - Merton's strain theory and material inequality

**Best fit:** Functionalism, strain, innovation

Welsh Government statistics for financial years ending 2023 to 2025 estimated that **22% of people in Wales lived in relative income poverty after housing costs**. Children in workless households had a substantially higher poverty risk than children in working households.

Merton's strain theory asks what happens when a society strongly values goals such as material success but access to legitimate means is unequal. His idea of **innovation** describes accepting the culturally valued goal while turning to illegitimate means.

**What this demonstrates:**

- material inequality provides a real context in which strain theory can be considered;
- structural theories connect individual behaviour to opportunity structures;
- poverty statistics do **not** prove that poverty causes crime. They provide context for testing the theory.

**Source:** [Welsh Government - Relative income poverty: April 2023 to March 2025](https://www.gov.wales/relative-income-poverty-april-2023-march-2025-official-statistics-development-html)

### Example S11 - Youth justice patterns and subcultural explanations

**Best fit:** Albert Cohen; Cloward and Ohlin; age and gender patterns

Youth Justice Board statistics for the year ending March 2025 show that **86% of children receiving a caution or sentence were boys**, and **76% were aged 15 to 17**, despite that age group forming a much smaller share of the 10 to 17 population.

**What this demonstrates:**

- there are strong age and gender patterns requiring sociological explanation;
- subcultural theorists can be used to explore peer groups, status, masculinity and access to legitimate and illegitimate opportunities;
- the statistics show the pattern, not its cause. The theory provides the explanation to be evaluated.

**Source:** [Youth Justice Board - Youth Justice Statistics 2024 to 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025)

### Example S12 - Corporate harm: Southern Water's £7.1 million pollution fine

**Best fit:** Marxism, corporate crime, power and selective attention

In July 2026 Southern Water was fined **£7.1 million** after repeated illegal sewage pollution incidents. The Environment Agency described preventable equipment failures and poor operational oversight. This followed an earlier £90 million fine in 2021.

**What this demonstrates:**

- harmful and criminal behaviour is not confined to stereotypical street offenders;
- Marxist sociology asks whether powerful organisations and working-class individuals receive equal visibility and control;
- corporate offending allows discussion of power, regulation, profit and who bears social harm.

**Source:** [Environment Agency - Southern Water fined £7.1 million for sewage pollution, 17 July 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/southern-water-find-71m-for-sewage-pollution)

### Example S13 - Interactionism and unequal criminal justice contact

**Best fit:** Becker, Cicourel, labelling and typifications

In 2024/25, Black people accounted for **13% of stop and searches** in England and Wales while Black people represented around 4% of the population in the 2021 Census comparison used by the Ministry of Justice. The report also notes geography, offence mix and other factors that affect interpretation.

**What this demonstrates:**

- official criminal justice patterns are not simply counts of underlying offending;
- interactionists ask how suspicion, discretion and institutional practices shape who becomes visible to the system;
- disproportionality is evidence to investigate, not proof by itself of one single explanation.

**Source:** [Ministry of Justice - Ethnicity and the Criminal Justice System 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/ethnicity-and-the-criminal-justice-system-2024/statistics-on-ethnicity-and-the-criminal-justice-system-2024-html)

### Example S14 - Realist approaches in practice: vehicle security and the Cardiff Model

**Best fit:** Right realism and left realism

A Home Office study concluded that vehicle crime fell around **80% from 1995**, with electronic immobilisers estimated to have contributed substantially to the reduction in stolen vehicles. This is a strong example of situational crime prevention: changing opportunities rather than changing offender values.

The Cardiff Model takes a different but still practical approach, combining police, health and local-authority information to identify violence hotspots and target prevention.

**What this demonstrates:**

- right-realist ideas can lead to target hardening and opportunity reduction;
- left-realist approaches can combine practical victim-focused prevention with attention to communities and social conditions;
- policy evidence can be used to compare what different theories expect to work.

**Sources:**

- [Home Office - Reducing criminal opportunity: vehicle security and vehicle crime](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reducing-criminal-opportunity-vehicle-security-and-vehicle-crime)
- [Cardiff University - Cardiff Model](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/documents/2665796-the-cardiff-model-for-violence-prevention)

### Example S15 - Feminist criminology: domestic abuse is gendered

**Best fit:** Heidensohn, feminist explanations, victimisation and control

The CSEW estimated that in the year ending March 2025 around **9.1% of females** and **6.5% of males** experienced domestic abuse. Police data supplied by 32 forces showed **72.1% of victims of domestic-abuse-related crimes were female**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- crime and victimisation have gendered patterns;
- feminist criminology broadens attention beyond male street offending to private-sphere violence and control;
- male victimisation remains real and should not be erased when describing the pattern.

**Source:** [ONS - Domestic abuse victim characteristics, year ending March 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/domesticabusevictimcharacteristicsenglandandwales/latest)

## 4. Contemporary social policy and crime control

### Example S16 - Situational prevention: electronic vehicle immobilisers

**Best fit:** Right realism, target hardening, evaluation of social policy

Home Office research concluded that improved vehicle security was a major factor in the long-term fall in vehicle crime. Electronic immobilisers were estimated to account for around **25% to 50% of the decline in stolen vehicles to 2013**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- policy can target opportunity instead of underlying motivation;
- an intervention can have measurable effects without accepting every assumption of right realism;
- evaluation should still consider displacement and adaptation by offenders.

**Source:** [Home Office - Reducing criminal opportunity: vehicle security and vehicle crime](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reducing-criminal-opportunity-vehicle-security-and-vehicle-crime)

### Example S17 - The Cardiff Model: prevention can change both harm and statistics

**Best fit:** Policy evaluation, multi-agency prevention, measurement

Evaluation of the Cardiff Model found a significant reduction in serious violence-related hospital admissions compared with similar cities. At the same time, police recording of less serious assaults increased.

**What this demonstrates:**

- a policy can reduce serious harm while recorded crime moves differently;
- evaluation needs more than one outcome measure;
- partnership between health, police and local government can target prevention using richer evidence.

**Source:** [Cardiff University - The Cardiff Model for Violence Prevention](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/documents/2665796-the-cardiff-model-for-violence-prevention)

### Example S18 - Youth diversion and avoiding unnecessary criminalisation

**Best fit:** Labelling theory, diversion, policy

The Edinburgh Study found that appropriately targeted diversionary strategies were associated with increased desistance from serious offending. Youth Justice Board data also show a long-term fall in the number of children cautioned or sentenced, with the number in 2024/25 around **65% lower than ten years earlier**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- labelling theory can influence real policy by supporting alternatives to formal criminalisation;
- avoiding a formal label may reduce some harmful consequences of justice-system contact;
- falling formal sanctions do not automatically mean offending fell by exactly the same amount.

**Sources:**

- [University of Edinburgh - Edinburgh Study impact](https://www.law.ed.ac.uk/research/impact-and-engagement/edinburgh-study-of-youth-transitions-and-crime)
- [Youth Justice Board - Youth Justice Statistics 2024 to 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025/youth-justice-statistics-2024-to-2025)

# Part B - Unit 4: Applied Methods of Sociological Enquiry

## 5. Designing sociological research

### Example S19 - British Social Attitudes: a real large-scale survey design

**Best fit:** Research design, quantitative methods, representativeness

The British Social Attitudes survey has tracked social and political attitudes since 1983. NatCen uses random probability sampling so the study can make stronger claims about the British population than a voluntary online poll.

**What this demonstrates:**

- research design follows from what researchers want to measure;
- repeated standardised questions allow trends to be compared over time;
- representative survey research requires more than simply collecting a large number of responses.

**Source:** [NatCen - British Social Attitudes](https://natcen.ac.uk/british-social-attitudes)

### Example S20 - Changing a question can change the statistic

**Best fit:** Operationalisation, validity, measurement

ONS redesigned the CSEW questions used to measure domestic abuse from the year ending March 2024. ONS warns that the new estimates cannot be directly compared with the old series because the questions and measurement changed.

**What this demonstrates:**

- operationalisation affects the data produced;
- a concept such as "domestic abuse" has to be turned into specific questions and indicators;
- improving validity can create a break in a time series.

**Source:** [ONS - Domestic abuse prevalence and trends: year ending March 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/domesticabuseprevalenceandtrendsenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)

### Example S21 - Method choice and mode: British Social Attitudes moved online

**Best fit:** Questionnaires, mode effects, reliability and practical constraints

British Social Attitudes was traditionally conducted face-to-face but moved to a push-to-web design during the COVID-19 period, with telephone completion available for people unable to complete online. NatCen used weighting and methodological checks to assess whether the changed mode altered the sample or results.

**What this demonstrates:**

- practical events can force methodological change;
- online research can introduce digital-exclusion and non-response issues;
- researchers can use weighting and comparison to reduce, but not magically remove, methodological problems.

**Source:** [NatCen - What makes British Social Attitudes a gold-standard survey?](https://natcen.ac.uk/what-makes-british-social-attitudes-gold-standard-social-survey)

### Example S22 - Mixed methods and longitudinal research: Edinburgh Study

**Best fit:** Longitudinal design, triangulation, primary and secondary data

The Edinburgh Study tracked a cohort of around 4,300 people over many years. It used repeated self-report questionnaires, interviews and administrative records from education, social work and criminal justice.

**What this demonstrates:**

- longitudinal research can study change across the life course;
- combining self-report and official records allows researchers to test whether different sources agree;
- rich designs produce stronger insight but are expensive, complex and vulnerable to attrition.

**Source:** [University of Edinburgh - About the Study](https://blogs.ed.ac.uk/edinstudy/about-the-study/)

### Example S23 - Random probability sampling and non-response

**Best fit:** Sampling, representativeness, weighting

NatCen selects British Social Attitudes addresses from the Postcode Address File using random probability methods. It also uses non-response weighting because selected people do not all take part.

**What this demonstrates:**

- probability sampling gives people a known chance of selection;
- a well-designed sample can still become biased through non-response;
- weighting is a methodological response to a problem, not proof that all bias has disappeared.

**Source:** [NatCen - British Social Attitudes methodology](https://natcen.ac.uk/what-makes-british-social-attitudes-gold-standard-social-survey)

### Example S24 - Sensitive research, consent and data linkage: Natsal

**Best fit:** Ethics, informed consent, sensitive topics, data linkage

The National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal) researches highly sensitive personal topics. Participant information explains that people can refuse biological samples and that separate permission is requested before linking survey responses to administrative records.

**What this demonstrates:**

- informed consent has to be specific, not assumed;
- sensitive research creates privacy, disclosure and distress issues;
- ethical safeguards can affect what data researchers are allowed to collect and link.

**Source:** [NatCen - Natsal participant information](https://natcen.ac.uk/natsal-study-information-leaflet-participants)

# Part C - Unit 4: Social Inequality

## 6. Contemporary patterns of inequality

### Example S25 - Poverty in Wales is persistent

**Best fit:** Social inequality, life chances, class

Welsh Government data for financial years ending 2023 to 2025 estimated that **22% of people in Wales lived in relative income poverty after housing costs**. A newer deep-material-poverty measure estimated that **10% of children** were in deep material poverty in financial years ending 2024 to 2025.

**What this demonstrates:**

- inequality is about material life chances as well as income differences;
- poverty can persist even in a wealthy society;
- class analysis needs evidence on housing, work, family and wealth, not only individual wages.

**Source:** [Welsh Government - Relative income poverty](https://www.gov.wales/relative-income-poverty-april-2023-march-2025-official-statistics-development-html)

### Example S26 - Wealth inequality is much larger than a simple income comparison

**Best fit:** Class inequality, wealth, life chances

ONS Wealth and Assets Survey data for April 2020 to March 2022 estimated median household wealth in Great Britain at **£293,700**. The wealthiest 10% of households had **£1,200,500 or more**, while the least wealthy 10% had **£16,500 or less**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- wealth and income are different dimensions of inequality;
- housing and pension assets can accumulate across the life course;
- class inequality can persist even when annual earnings alone appear less unequal.

**Source:** [ONS - Household total wealth in Great Britain](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/incomeandwealth/bulletins/totalwealthingreatbritain/latest)

### Example S27 - Gender pay gap remains measurable

**Best fit:** Gender inequality, labour market, occupational segregation

ONS reported that the UK full-time employee gender pay gap was **6.9% in April 2025**, down from 7.1% in 2024. Welsh Government data likewise show a long-term narrowing of the full-time gender pay difference in Wales.

**What this demonstrates:**

- gender inequality can persist while changing over time;
- a pay gap describes a population pattern and does not by itself prove direct unequal pay for the same job;
- explanations can include occupational segregation, caring responsibilities, progression, discrimination and working patterns.

**Sources:**

- [ONS - Gender pay gap in the UK: 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/earningsandworkinghours/bulletins/genderpaygapintheuk/2025)
- [Welsh Government - Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings 2025](https://www.gov.wales/annual-survey-hours-and-earnings-2025-html)

### Example S28 - Ethnic inequality in Wales varies by outcome

**Best fit:** Ethnicity, employment, pay and poverty

Welsh Government reporting for 2024 found Black, Asian and Minority Ethnic employees in Wales earned on average **3.6% less per hour** than White employees, while warning that the ethnicity pay-gap series is volatile. The same Wellbeing of Wales evidence reported a substantially higher risk of relative income poverty among households headed by someone from a Black, Asian or Minority Ethnic background than among White-headed households.

**What this demonstrates:**

- ethnic inequality can appear across several institutions and outcomes;
- broad ethnic categories can hide major differences within groups;
- short-term changes should not be overinterpreted when sample sizes are small or estimates are volatile.

**Source:** [Welsh Government - Wellbeing of Wales 2025: a more equal Wales](https://www.gov.wales/wellbeing-wales-2025-more-equal-wales-html)

### Example S29 - Age inequality: NEET rates in Wales

**Best fit:** Age, transition to adulthood, employment and education

Welsh Government estimated that **17.0% of 16 to 24 year olds in Wales were not in education, employment or training (NEET)** in the year ending December 2025. The release also shows variation by disability, ethnicity and region.

**What this demonstrates:**

- age affects exposure to particular labour-market risks;
- inequality at the transition from school to work can shape later life chances;
- intersectionality matters because age interacts with disability, ethnicity and place.

**Source:** [Welsh Government - Young people NEET: 2025](https://www.gov.wales/young-people-not-education-employment-or-training-neet-2025-html)

## 7. Theories and explanations of inequality

### Example S30 - Davis and Moore: do unequal rewards really match social importance?

**Best fit:** Functionalism

Davis and Moore argue that unequal rewards can motivate people to train for difficult or socially important roles. A useful real-world test is to compare social contribution, scarcity of skills and actual reward across occupations such as care workers, teachers, nurses, finance professionals and senior executives.

**What this demonstrates:**

- functionalism gives inequality a possible social function;
- the theory can be evaluated by asking who decides which roles are most important;
- market pay does not automatically measure social necessity.

**Evidence link:** [ONS - Earnings and working hours](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/earningsandworkinghours)

### Example S31 - Marx: wealth concentration and class conflict

**Best fit:** Marxism

A memorable Marx and Engels quotation is:

> "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."

Modern wealth evidence gives a way to test whether class divisions remain materially important. ONS found the wealthiest tenth of Great British households held at least £1.2 million in household wealth in the latest detailed distribution release, while the least wealthy tenth held £16,500 or less.

**What this demonstrates:**

- Marxism directs attention to ownership, accumulated assets and unequal economic power;
- persistent wealth concentration provides contemporary material for evaluating class theories;
- inequality data alone do not prove all aspects of Marx's theory.

**Sources:**

- [Marxists Internet Archive - Marx/Engels on historical materialism](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/subject/hist-mat/index.htm)
- [ONS - Household total wealth in Great Britain](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/incomeandwealth/bulletins/totalwealthingreatbritain/latest)

### Example S32 - Weber: class is more than ownership alone

**Best fit:** Weberian theory, class, status and party

A useful short Weber quotation is:

> "Class situation is, in this sense, ultimately market situation."

Weber's approach allows sociologists to examine income and employment alongside status, qualifications, credentials and organised political power.

**Real-world illustration:** Two people may have similar income but very different job security, professional status, pension wealth, networks or political influence. Equally, a highly respected occupation may not be among the highest paid.

**What this demonstrates:**

- inequality is multidimensional;
- market position shapes life chances but does not exhaust social stratification;
- Weber can explain combinations of class, status and power that a simple two-class model may miss.

**Source:** [Springer - Weber on class, status groups and politics](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43545-025-01183-w)

### Example S33 - Feminism: paid work, safety and domestic abuse

**Best fit:** Feminist explanations of inequality

Current evidence shows gender inequality across different spheres rather than one single outcome. The UK full-time gender pay gap remained 6.9% in 2025, while the CSEW estimated women were significantly more likely than men to experience domestic and partner abuse in the year ending March 2025.

**What this demonstrates:**

- feminist analysis connects economic inequality with power and safety in private as well as public life;
- gender inequality cannot be reduced to wages alone;
- different feminist perspectives can disagree about whether capitalism, patriarchy, culture, law or their interaction is most important.

**Sources:**

- [ONS - Gender pay gap 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/earningsandworkinghours/bulletins/genderpaygapintheuk/2025)
- [ONS - Domestic abuse victim characteristics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/domesticabusevictimcharacteristicsenglandandwales/latest)

### Example S34 - New Right arguments meet the evidence on in-work poverty

**Best fit:** New Right, welfare, individual responsibility

Welsh poverty statistics show that employment strongly reduces poverty risk, but do not support a simple divide between "working" and "poor". In financial years ending 2023 to 2025, children in households where all adults worked had a lower poverty risk than those in workless households, but **22% of children in all-working households were still in relative income poverty**.

**What this demonstrates:**

- employment matters, supporting part of the New Right emphasis on labour-market participation;
- work does not automatically remove poverty, which supports structural criticisms around low pay, housing costs and household composition;
- good evaluation tests a theory against evidence that both supports and challenges it.

**Source:** [Welsh Government - Relative income poverty](https://www.gov.wales/relative-income-poverty-april-2023-march-2025-official-statistics-development-html)

### Example S35 - Postmodernism versus persistent material inequality

**Best fit:** Postmodernism, identity, fragmentation, class debate

Postmodern approaches stress diversity, changing identities and consumption. However, current Welsh health data show highly durable material inequalities. In 2022 to 2024, male life expectancy at birth was **73.6 years in the most deprived areas of Wales and 81.9 years in the least deprived**. Healthy life expectancy showed an even larger gap.

**What this demonstrates:**

- identity may be more diverse without material inequality disappearing;
- deprivation remains strongly associated with life chances such as health and longevity;
- this is powerful counter-evidence to any claim that class and material position have become unimportant.

**Source:** [ONS - Healthy life expectancy by deprivation, England and Wales: 2022 to 2024](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/healthandsocialcare/healthinequalities/bulletins/healthylifeexpectancybynationalareadeprivationenglandandwales/between2013to2015and2022to2024)

# Part E - Short quotation bank

Quotes should support understanding, not replace explanation. Keep them short and explain what each means.

### Howard Becker - labelling

Use for: labelling, social reaction, secondary deviance.

Use for: moral panic, folk devils, media and social reaction.

Use for: Marxism, class conflict, power and ownership.

## Important exam-use rule

A current statistic or news story is strongest when it is attached to a sociological idea:

**Point → named sociologist/theory → evidence/example → explain what it shows → limitation/counterargument.**

Do not turn an example into a claim of causation unless the research genuinely establishes causation.
